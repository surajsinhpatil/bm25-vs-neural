\section{Introduction}
Dense retrievers trained on general web data are often assumed to beat lexical retrieval. We test
this on technical text where exact strings matter: computer-security abstracts from arXiv (cs.CR),
which are full of identifiers (CVE numbers, protocol versions), homonyms (\emph{oracle},
\emph{poisoning}, \emph{fingerprinting}) and conditions such as exclusions and date limits.
We compare our identifier-aware BM25, two dense models used without fine-tuning (E5-base-v2 and
SPECTER2), and their Reciprocal Rank Fusion hybrids. Research questions:
\textbf{RQ1} Does a dense retriever beat BM25 overall?
\textbf{RQ2} How does this depend on the query type?
\textbf{RQ3} Does the science-specific SPECTER2 beat the general E5?
\textbf{RQ4} Does a hybrid beat both of its components?
\textbf{RQ5} How sensitive is BM25 to $k_1$, $b$ and tokenisation?

\section{Related work}
BM25 \cite{robertson} remains a strong zero-shot baseline: in BEIR \cite{beir} it beat many dense
models outside their training domain. E5 \cite{e5} is trained contrastively on large weakly
supervised pair data and then fine-tuned. SPECTER2 \cite{specter2} is trained on scientific
citation data, with task adapters for proximity and ad-hoc search. RRF \cite{rrf} fuses ranked
lists using ranks only. Bruch et al.\ \cite{bruch} compare RRF with convex score combination.
Dense models are known to handle negation poorly \cite{nevir}. Our design for significance tests
follows Smucker et al.\ \cite{smucker}; for assessor disagreement it follows Voorhees \cite{voorhees}.

\section{Data, queries and systems}
\textbf{Corpus.} We use all \corpusN{} arXiv papers with cs.CR as primary or cross-listed category
and a first version in 2016--2025. Withdrawn papers and duplicate titles are removed and LaTeX is
cleaned (SHA-256 \texttt{\corpusSha\ldots}). A document is its title plus abstract.
\textbf{Queries.} 50 queries, 10 per category: exact identifier (e.g.\ ``Spectre variant~1 bounds check
bypass (CVE-2017-5753)''), homonym (``padding oracle attacks on CBC mode''), negation (``anonymous
communication systems that are \emph{not} based on Tor''), time constraint (``attacks on TLS~1.3
published after its 2018 standardisation'') and paraphrase (``copying a deployed model by repeatedly
querying its prediction interface''). Each query has a description and written relevance criteria.
\textbf{Systems.} (1)~BM25 ($k_1{=}1.2$, $b{=}0.75$) with an identifier-aware analyzer. It keeps
\texttt{cve-2021-44228} or \texttt{tls1.3} as single tokens and also indexes their parts;
Lucene's stopwords are removed (including ``not'') and the Porter stemmer is applied.
(2)~E5-base-v2 and (3)~SPECTER2 (proximity adapter for documents, ad-hoc query adapter for queries),
both with exact cosine search. (4, 5)~RRF ($k{=}60$, top 100 of each run) of BM25 with E5 and with
SPECTER2. Nothing was tuned on our queries.

\section{Evaluation protocol}
\textbf{Pooling and judging.} The top 10 of all five runs form the pool: \finPairs{} query--document
pairs, about 27 per query. Every query was judged independently by two of the four authors, with the
six annotator pairs spread evenly. The judging pages showed no system names or ranks, and showed dates
only for time queries. Grades: 0 not relevant, 1 on topic but does not answer the query or breaks a
condition, 2 relevant, 3 highly relevant. All 2{,}678 judgments were collected and audited: none is
missing, duplicated or outside the pool.
\textbf{Agreement.} Weighted Cohen's $\kappa$ = \finKappaLin{} (linear) and \finKappaQuad{}
(quadratic); $\kappa$ on binary relevance (grade $\geq 2$) = \finKappaBin{}; exact agreement
\finExact{}. This meets the protocol's target of $\kappa\geq 0.6$. \finConflicts{} pairs conflict:
\finZeroThree{} are 0 vs 3, and \finCross{} fall on opposite sides of the relevance line.
\textbf{Adjudication.} All conflicts were resolved in a team discussion and recorded by one author;
every final grade is one of the two submitted grades. There was no independent adjudicator: the
annotators of a pair took part in its discussion. The recorder was one of the two annotators in 160
conflicts and kept his own grade in 41\% of them (51--55\% for the other three). 27 decisions were
taken from the title or by a ``lower grade when unsure'' rule, as their rationales record. The qrels were
then frozen with a SHA-256 checksum and a provenance file.
\textbf{Metrics.} The primary metric is nDCG@10 with gain equal to the grade. P@5, MRR@10 and MAP@10
use grade $\geq 2$. Unjudged documents count as non-relevant; Hole@10 reports how often they occur.
Our metric code matches \texttt{trec\_eval} (via \texttt{pytrec\_eval}) on every query of every run,
with a maximum difference of $3\times10^{-16}$.
\textbf{Significance.} Paired randomisation tests over queries (100{,}000 permutations) on nDCG@10 for
all 10 system pairs, with Holm--Bonferroni correction. Per-category results come with 95\% bootstrap
CIs and are exploratory, since each category has only 10 queries.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Validation of the implementation (SciFact)}\label{sec:sci}
This is not a cs.CR result. On BEIR SciFact (300 queries) with the same settings as Pyserini, our BM25
differs from Lucene by $\Delta$nDCG@10 = \sciDiff{} and shares \sciOverlap\% of its top-10 documents.
On the same collection E5 (\sciEfive) beats BM25 (\sciBM) significantly ($p_{\text{Holm}}$ =
\sciPBmtwofiveEfive), while SPECTER2 (\sciSpec) does not.

\subsection{Main results on cs.CR}

\inputor{tables/fin_sig.tex}{}
Hole@10 is 0 for every system: every document in every top 10 was judged (Table~\ref{tab:fmain}).
\textbf{RQ1:} E5 and BM25 are statistically indistinguishable (E5 +0.023, 95\% CI $-$0.038 to +0.084,
$p_{\text{Holm}}=0.93$). E5 is better on 24 queries and BM25 on 23. The data give no support for the
claim that a general dense model beats BM25 on this collection.
\textbf{RQ3:} SPECTER2 is the weakest system, 0.147 below BM25 and 0.170 below E5 (both
$p_{\text{Holm}}\leq 0.005$), and significantly below both hybrids. Its citation-based training does
not transfer to short ad-hoc queries.
\textbf{RQ4:} BM25+E5 is the best system (nDCG@10 0.744). It is significantly better than BM25 (+0.055,
$p_{\text{Holm}}=0.023$; better on 32 queries, worse on 16) and than BM25+SPECTER2 (+0.063,
$p_{\text{Holm}}=0.031$), but not than E5 (+0.031, $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.59$). So the hybrid does not
beat \emph{both} of its components. BM25+SPECTER2 recovers most of SPECTER2's loss but does not beat
BM25. Excluding the query with no relevant document leaves every significance decision unchanged.
\textbf{How much depends on adjudication} (Table~\ref{tab:fsens}, computed from the two annotators'
grades alone). No way of resolving the conflicts can reverse the four SPECTER2 deficits, and they are
significant in 99--100\% of \finSensN{} random adjudications. BM25+E5 has the highest mean in
\finSensFirst\% of them. By contrast, its significant advantage over BM25 appears in only 7\% of random
adjudications, and the sign of that difference could even flip under some adjudications. That
significance is therefore a property of our adjudication rather than a robust effect, and we treat it
with caution.

\subsection{Error analysis}
\inputor{tables/fin_g1.tex}{}
\textbf{Conditions are ignored.} On negation queries, 81\% of BM25's top 10 are graded 1, mostly
papers that break the exclusion. The figure is 60\% for E5 and 71\% for BM25+E5 (Table~\ref{tab:fg1}).
BM25 removes ``not'' as a stopword: for ``anonymous communication systems that are not based on Tor'',
seven of its top 10 titles name Tor (e.g.\ ``Analyzing Trends in Tor'', ``DarkHorse: \ldots{} Tor Onion
Services''), all graded 1. E5's top 10 includes DC-nets, Atom and Pirates (grade 3). On time queries,
40--54\% of each system's top 10 is graded 1. Counting from first-version dates, 30--38\% of each
system's top 10 breaks the date condition; a rule-based date filter (stretch goal S2) removes all of these.
\textbf{Paraphrases defeat lexical matching.} For ``programs that lock a victim's files and demand
payment to unlock them'', BM25 ranks ``Scalable Time-Lock Puzzle'' and a digital-payment framework near
the top (nDCG@10 0.18), while E5 returns ransomware papers (0.82).
\textbf{Identifiers defeat dense matching.} For ``replay attacks on TLS~1.3 0-RTT early data'', E5's top
five are replay attacks in unrelated fields such as space launch and sharded ledgers (0.18), while BM25
finds TLS~1.3 papers (0.82).
\textbf{The two systems complement each other.} Of the relevant documents in the BM25 and E5 top 10s,
\finBoth{} are found by both, \finOnlyBM{} only by BM25 and \finOnlyE{} only by E5. This is why fusion
helps. Full examples are in \texttt{deliverables/final/analysis/error\_analysis.md}.

\subsection{Efficiency}
\inputor{tables/efficiency_prelim.tex}{}
All numbers are measured from the run logs (Table~\ref{tab:eff}). BM25 builds its index in 20\,s on one
CPU into 29\,MB and answers in 1.6\,ms. Each dense index is 116\,MB (4$\times$ larger) and takes about
3\,min of T4 GPU time. Dense queries take 17--30\,ms, mostly query encoding; exact search alone takes
6--7\,ms. BM25+E5 therefore buys its effectiveness at roughly 12$\times$ the query cost of BM25.

\subsection{BM25 sensitivity (RQ5, exploratory)}\label{sec:rq5}
Both experiments use the corpus, the 50 queries, the frozen qrels and the metric code of the main
experiment. They ran on Kaggle (commit \texttt{\rqCommit}) after three checks: the qrels match their frozen
checksum, the rebuilt corpus is the frozen one (39{,}613 documents, SHA-256 \texttt{\rqCorpusSha\ldots}), and
the default BM25 run rebuilt from it reproduces the pooled run (same top 10 for all 50 queries, scores within
$\rqReproMax$). Outputs, metadata and checksums are in \texttt{deliverables/rq5/}.

\textbf{Pool bias.} Only one BM25 configuration, the default ($k_1$=1.2, $b$=0.75, identifier-aware
analyzer), was in the judging pool. Every other setting puts documents nobody judged into its top 10, and
nDCG@10 counts them as non-relevant, which favours the default by construction. We therefore also report
Hole@10 (the share of unjudged documents in the top 10) and condensed nDCG@10 \cite{sakai}, which removes
unjudged documents from the ranking before cutting at 10. Condensed nDCG@10 is not a bound or an unbiased
estimate either: it can overrate runs that were not pooled, and in our data it rises with Hole@10. We treat
a result as robust only if both metrics agree. Across the 132 settings, Hole@10 correlates with nDCG@10 at $r$=\rqRHoleN{} and
with condensed nDCG@10 at $r$=\rqRHoleC{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:rq5}). Away from the default, both metrics mostly
reflect how many unjudged documents a setting retrieves.

\textbf{$k_1$ and $b$} (Table~\ref{tab:rq5sweep}). On nDCG@10, \rqAbove{} of the 132 settings score above the
default. \rqWithinN{} settings lie within 0.01 of it ($k_1$ \rqPlateauK, $b$
\rqPlateauB), and \rqWithinTwo\% lie within 0.02. Low $k_1$ looks clearly worse ($k_1\leq0.4$:
\rqLowK), but these are the settings with the most unjudged documents. Condensed nDCG@10 orders the grid
almost the other way round. Its best setting is \rqCondBest{} (\rqCondBestC), where \rqCondBestHole{} of
the top 10 is unjudged and nDCG@10 is \rqCondBestN. Neither ranking of settings far from the default can be
trusted without judging their documents. The default being the best setting on nDCG@10 is what pool bias
predicts, so it is not evidence that the default is optimal.
The only tuned estimate is cross-validated: 5 folds stratified by category, with $(k_1, b)$ chosen on four
folds and scored on the fifth. It reaches \rqCV{} against \rqDefault{} for the default ($\Delta$ =
\rqCVDiff, 95\% CI \rqCVlo{} to \rqCVhi, $p$=\rqCVp; better on \rqCVwins{} queries, equal on \rqCVties,
worse on \rqCVlosses). On condensed nDCG@10 the difference vanishes (\rqCVcDiff, $p$=\rqCVcp). Because the
metrics disagree, we do not claim that tuning hurts; we only conclude that it did not help. The folds chose
\rqCVchosen, and \rqSeedN{} other fold assignments give \rqSeedMin--\rqSeedMax.

\textbf{Tokenisation} (Table~\ref{tab:rq5tok}). \emph{Identifier handling} made no measurable difference.
Replacing the identifier-aware analyzer with the Lucene-like one changes nDCG@10 by \rqPlainDiff{}
($p_{\text{Holm}}$=\rqPlainP), \rqPlainTies{} of 50 queries are unchanged, and condensed nDCG@10 agrees.
This is the one comparison almost free of pool bias: Hole@10 is \rqPlainHole{} (\rqPlainUnjudged{}
unjudged documents in total). Even on the identifier queries both analyzers score \rqIdIdent. A plausible
reason, which we did not test, is that these queries also contain distinctive words (``Log4Shell'',
``Spectre''), and the Lucene-like analyzer still indexes the pieces of each identifier.
\emph{Stemming} cannot be judged with the current qrels. Removing it lowers nDCG@10 by \rqNostemDiff{}
($p_{\text{Holm}}$ \rqNostemP), but raises Hole@10 to \rqNostemHole, and condensed nDCG@10 moves the other
way (\rqNostemCDiff, $p_{\text{Holm}}$=\rqNostemCP). Both differences are significant and in opposite
directions, so we draw no conclusion; the same holds without the identifier handling.
\emph{Keeping stopwords} (including ``not'') gives \rqNostopDiff{} on nDCG@10 ($p_{\text{Holm}}$=\rqNostopP)
and \rqNostopCDiff{} on condensed nDCG@10 ($p_{\text{Holm}}$=\rqNostopCP), so no conclusion either. On the
negation queries it scores \rqNostopNeg{} against \rqIdNeg, so keeping ``not'' as a term shows no benefit
for negation here (with \rqNostopHole{} of its top 10 unjudged overall). Judging the \rqUnjudgedUnion{} unjudged query--document pairs in the variants' top
10s would resolve these comparisons.

\textbf{Answer to RQ5.} Near the default, BM25 is insensitive to $k_1$ and $b$: there is a broad plateau,
and cross-validated tuning gave no gain. Identifier-aware tokenisation made no measurable difference on
our queries. Whether stemming, stopword removal or settings far from the default matter cannot be decided
with the current judgments.

\section{Limitations}
(1) There are 50 queries, 10 per category, so category effects are exploratory. (2) The pool contains
only the top 10 of five systems. Hole@10 is 0 for these systems, but recall-oriented metrics, and any
system outside the pool, would be biased. (3) Adjudication was a team discussion without an independent
adjudicator, and 27 decisions rest on titles or a ``lower when unsure'' rule. The hybrid's significant
advantage over BM25 is sensitive to such choices (Table~\ref{tab:fsens}). (4) No relevant document
(grade $\geq 2$) was found for one query (AES-GCM nonce reuse); excluding it changes no conclusion.
(5) The authors wrote the queries and judged them. (6) The dense models may have seen arXiv abstracts
during pre-training. (7) A grade of 1 earns nDCG gain, so systems partly get credit for documents that
break a condition. With an exploratory strict gain (no gain for grade 1, \texttt{sNDCG@10}), the order
is the same except that BM25+SPECTER2 (0.533) moves just above BM25 (0.523). (8) RQ5 is exploratory: only
the default BM25 configuration was pooled, so other settings and analyzers are scored with unjudged
documents in their top 10 (up to 30\%), and ordinary and condensed nDCG@10 disagree for most of them.
The cross-validation reuses the same 50 queries.

\section{Conclusions}
On security abstracts, a general dense retriever used without fine-tuning does not beat a careful BM25
overall, and the science-specific SPECTER2 is clearly worse than both. BM25 and E5 fail on different
queries: BM25 on paraphrases and negation, E5 on exact identifiers. As a result, their RRF fusion is the
best system (nDCG@10 0.744), significantly better than BM25, although that margin depends on how the
judgment conflicts were adjudicated. It is not better than E5 alone, and it costs about 12$\times$ the
query time of BM25. Every system largely ignores exclusions and date limits; handling them explicitly
(S2) looks more promising than switching models. BM25 itself is robust near its default parameters, and
identifier-aware tokenisation made no measurable difference. Whether stemming or stopword removal matter
remains open until the documents those variants retrieve are judged.
